Title: Chain-of-Thought Oversight Requires Separate Evidence for Faithfulness and Monitorability
Authors: Sichao Li, Sai Ma, Xiyang Hu, Chudi Zhong, Tongliang Liu
ID: 2611.01620
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01620

Abstract:
A common assumption in chain-of-thought (CoT) oversight is that a visible
reasoning trace makes model behavior easier to monitor. We argue that this
assumption can conflate distinct properties along a chain linking a model's
internal computation, reasoning artifacts, outputs, and monitor judgments. We
identify four properties along this chain: reasoning visibility (whether a trace
is accessible), mechanistic faithfulness (whether a trace reflects internal
computation), mechanistic localizability (whether a compact causal mechanism can
be isolated), and behavioral monitorability (whether a monitor can detect the
target behavior). Two complementary case studies across three open-weight
reasoning models show why these distinctions matter. In the security
vulnerability reasoning study, probes and activation ablations provide evidence
of internal representation and causal influence, but a monitor with access to
the reference answer does not outperform the majority-class baseline in a held-
out test. In authority-hint factual reasoning study, a monitor correctly
distinguishes changed from unchanged answers, but seven mechanistic analyses do
not isolate a compact causal mechanism. These results show that evidence for one
claim does not establish the others. We therefore propose an evidential-chain
reporting standard. Each claim carries a licensing statement that specifies its
target property, access tier, and artifact, and labels each required link as
supported, unsupported, or assumed. These assessments determine whether the
conclusion is licensed, conditionally licensed, or not licensed.
