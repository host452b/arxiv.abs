Title: Learning What to Change: State-Verified Reinforcement Learning for Educational Agents
Authors: Shengyao Wang, Siyuan Su, Siyu Song, Mengsong Wu, Yiming Luo, Wenbo Wu, Hao Hao, Aimin Zhou
ID: 2611.30559
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30559

Abstract:
Persistent tutoring agents do more than answer questions: they manage course
files, practice records, and learner profiles that govern future lessons. In
such stateful settings, knowing what to teach does not guarantee knowing what to
change. We identify a pervasive failure mode, content-state decoupling, where
agents deliver pedagogically sound responses while silently corrupting workspace
state through unwarranted updates, missed prerequisites, or unintended side
effects. Text-level judges overlook these state errors, whereas isolated tool-
call checks break down because whether a write action is valid depends jointly
on the learner request, prior state, and execution order. We propose State-
Verified Reinforcement Learning, which supervises online policy optimization
directly through verifiable workspace transitions. Within isolated sandboxes
that snapshot state around every tool call, a deterministic verifier audits tool
effects, workflow ordering, and native auto-sync side effects, pairing graded
partial credit with a hard gate that zeroes episode reward upon any critical
violation. Whenever no critical violation occurs, an evidence-grounded judge
scores actual saved artifacts rather than agent claims. We pair 1,000 verifiable
training instances with an independently authored, human-reviewed evaluation
benchmark of 60 tasks spanning 11 educational categories and 16 native tools.
After online GRPO training, Qwen3.8-27B raises category-weighted strict success
on our benchmark from 51.47% to 80.74% (+29.27 points), cutting content-pass-
only execution failures from 45.6% to 13.7% and required state-check failures
from 63 to 1 across 180 trials. The resulting policy outperforms seven reference
baselines by over 20 points while reducing output tokens by 17-39% across seven
of eight external benchmarks without regressing on general reasoning or tool
use.
