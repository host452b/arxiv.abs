Title: Balanced Voronoi Partitions, Self-Approaching Sequences, and Applications to Efficiently Navigable Graphs
Authors: Jonathan Conroy, Mohammadreza Daneshvaramoli, Cameron Musco, Christopher Musco, Jack Spalding-Jamieson
ID: 2611.32667
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32667

Abstract:
We study the following geometric question: given a set $X$ of points in
$d$-dimensional Euclidean space, is it always possible to pick $a,b \in X$ such
that their induced Voronoi partition is approximately balanced? I.e., letting
$X_a,X_b \subseteq X$ be the sets of points closer to $a$ and $b$ respectively,
can we pick $a$ and $b$ such that $\min(|X_a|,|X_b|) \ge α\cdot |X|$ for some
$α> 0$? We show that balance $α= Ω(1/d)$ is always achievable, which is tight up
to a constant. The proof is via a reduction to a natural question about self-
approaching sequences (SASs): what is the longest sequence of points such that
each successive point gets closer to all other points? We show that if there is
no balanced Voronoi partition with balance $α$, then one can find an SAS of
length $Ω(1/α)$. We then prove that in $d$-dimensional Euclidean space, any SAS
has length at most $O(d)$, implying our balanced partitioning result. We discuss
extensions beyond Euclidean space: surprisingly, for the $\ell_p$ norm for any
constant $p \neq 2$, we show that one cannot achieve balance better than
$\mathrm{exp}(-Ω(d))$. However, a $1/\mathrm{poly}(d)$ bound does hold for point
sets with bounded aspect ratio. While we seem to be the first to study the exact
problems above, both are closely related to work on navigable search graphs,
which admit efficient routing via greedy search, and form the backbone of many
modern near neighbor search systems. Results on navigable graphs from the 1990s
can be used to give a bound of $α= 1/2^{O(d)}$ for balanced Voronoi
partitioning. Our bound exponentially improves this, to $Ω(1/d)$. Our bound also
yields the first construction of navigable graphs under a natural variant of
classic greedy routing, with routing time $O(d \log |X|)$. All existing
constructions require $\mathrm{exp}(d)$ routing time.
