Title: Convex splitting by binary quantum hypothesis testing
Authors: Mark M. Wilde
ID: 2611.25496
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25496

Abstract:
How many independent quantum systems does it take to hide one system that is
correlated with a reference? An observer can measure all the systems together
with the reference to detect the correlation. We turn any such measurement into
binary tests on a single system and the reference. For each test we bound the
probability of declaring a signal when an independent pair was prepared.
Averaging the largest signal acceptance probabilities allowed by these bounds
controls the normalized trace distance in the convex-split lemma. A second bound
gives a sufficient number of systems by minimizing the hypothesis-testing
relative entropy over the state of the reference. Both bounds hold for density
operators on separable Hilbert spaces. Comparing two distributions for the
location of the hidden system gives one-shot bounds for private communication
and one-way secret key distillation, including the public information available
to an eavesdropper. For a fixed finite-alphabet model with separable quantum
outputs and positive information advantage and variances, we recover the known
Gaussian corrections to the attainable number of private or key bits from $n$
independent repetitions and bound the remaining loss by $\log_2 n+O(1)$ at fixed
errors. The position-based key distillation protocol attains this bound with
$O(n)$ public bits.
