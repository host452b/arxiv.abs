Title: Revisiting Continual Learning in Language Models: A Three-Interface Diagnosis of Stability and Plasticity
Authors: Keuntae Kim, Beomseok Lee, Hyunwoo Kim, Yong Suk Choi
ID: 2611.30151
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30151

Abstract:
Continual learning in language models is commonly studied in the incremental
learning (IL) setting, where a model learns a sequence of tasks and is evaluated
through its final classifier or language-model (LM) head. This interface,
however, does not indicate where forgetting occurs: a drop in accuracy may
reflect representation degradation in the backbone, readout mismatch in the
head, or both. We therefore argue that backward transfer (BWT) and forward
transfer (FWT) should be interpreted as interface-dependent quantities rather
than as single modellevel properties. We study three evaluation interfaces: the
trained head, which is updated sequentially with the backbone; a probe newly
trained on frozen backbone features; and a classifier-free backbone diagnostic.
They measure deployed performance, recoverability under a new readout, and
representation separability, respectively. Across class-incremental experiments
with encoder and decoder language backbones, plus task-incremental sanity
checks, conclusions about forgetting and plasticity change with the interface:
the trained head often indicates severe forgetting, the probe near-complete
recoverability, and the backbone diagnostic intermediate representation drift
with a consistent negative relation between stability and plasticity. This
relation holds for clustering-free diagnostics such as nearest-class-mean
accuracy and across five clustering algorithms and three metrics. Finally, Just
LM-Head Tuning (JLT), a head-only intervention on a fixed incrementally trained
backbone, recovers 96-99% of the gap to joint training for replayand
distillation-based methods but only about three quarters for plain sequential
fine-tuning, suggesting that many failures attributed to catastrophic forgetting
reflect head-backbone mismatch plus partial representation drift. Our results
call for reporting where forgetting is measured, not only how much.
