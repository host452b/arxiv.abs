Title: Influence of mobile games on cognitive development among Somali children
Authors: Ibrahim Abubakar Abdi, Mohamed Ismael Iman, Mohamed Abdullahi Ibrahim
ID: openalex.W7225379289
URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1998358

Abstract:
Introduction and aims: The increasing availability of smartphones and internet
access in Somalia has contributed to the growing use of mobile games among
children. Mobile gaming may influence cognitive development positively or
negatively depending on gaming habits and supervision. This study aimed to
assess the influence of mobile games on cognitive development among Somali
children aged 6-15 years. Methods: A descriptive cross-sectional survey was
conducted among 200 Somali children, with their accompanying parents or
guardians, recruited from selected primary and secondary schools in Mogadishu by
multistage sampling. A structured questionnaire recorded gaming habits, parental
supervision, and perceived positive and negative cognitive effects on a 5-point
Likert scale. Data were summarised as frequencies, percentages, means, and
standard deviations (SD). Results: More than half of the children (55.0%) played
daily, and 70.0% played for 1-2 h per day. Action (45.0%) and sports (35.0%)
games predominated, educational games were played by 5.0%, and only 25.0% of
children were always supervised. Positive-effect items had an overall mean of
3.71; agreement was highest for "games make learning more interesting" (4.00 ±
0.95). Negative-effect items had an overall mean of 3.56. Among perceived
cognitive and academic difficulties (domain mean 3.54), reduced study time
scored highest (3.80 ± 1.13). Indicators of problematic gaming had a domain mean
of 3.66, and 60.0% of children found it difficult to stop playing. Fatigue and
reduced school engagement had a domain mean of 3.55. Discussion: Somali children
in this sample perceived mobile gaming as having both beneficial and harmful
effects on cognitive development. The evidence is descriptive and self-reported
and does not establish causation. Balanced gaming, closer parental supervision,
and greater use of educational games are recommended, and longitudinal studies
with objective cognitive measures are needed.
