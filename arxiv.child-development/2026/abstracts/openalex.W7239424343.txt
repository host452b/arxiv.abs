Title: Big data, nutrition, and mental health: implications for artificial intelligence based interventions
Authors: Kingsley Arua Kalu, Grace Ataguba, Oladapo Oyebode, Alexandria Mekanna, Jesse Enebi Usman, Ejemai Eboreime, Rita Orji
ID: openalex.W7239424343
URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1976560

Abstract:
Background Nutrition is a modifiable factor associated with mental health. Big
data, machine learning (ML), digital health and multimodal sensing offer
opportunities to characterize these relationships, but evidence remains
fragmented. This review examined nutrition–mental-health findings, analytical
approaches and implications for artificial intelligence interventions. Methods
Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched six databases, with searches completed
on 17 February 2026. Of 42,840 records, 2,656 duplicates were removed, 40,184
records were screened, 4,285 were assessed for eligibility and 35 studies were
included. Study characteristics, dietary exposures, mental-health outcomes,
analytical methods and quantitative estimates were extracted and synthesized
descriptively and narratively. Two researchers assessed methodological quality
and risk of bias using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), version 2018;
interrater agreement was Cohen’s κ = 0.78. One Mendelian-randomization report
received a separate domain-based appraisal. Results Depression was the most
frequently studied outcome (24 studies), followed by anxiety (11) and stress
(7). Healthier dietary patterns and nutrient-dense foods were generally
associated with favorable mental-health outcomes, whereas ultra-processed foods,
sugary foods/beverages and irregular eating patterns were more frequently
associated with adverse outcomes. Associations varied by population and design.
Twenty-seven studies (77.1%) reported relevant quantitative estimates. Examples
included lower depression odds for the highest versus lowest tertile of a
vegetable–egg–beans–milk pattern (adjusted OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.49–0.87) and no
clear association per 10-point higher AHEI diet-quality score (OR 1.16, 95% CI
0.96–1.39). Estimates were not pooled because substantial heterogeneity in study
designs, dietary exposures, outcome measures and effect definitions precluded a
meaningful pooled effect. MMAT profiles covered six randomized and 28 non-
randomized studies; frequent concerns included representativeness, incomplete
outcomes and residual confounding. ML applications primarily addressed
depression; differences in targets and validation procedures prevented cross-
study performance ranking. Conclusion Big-data approaches broaden evidence on
nutrition and mental health, but observational associations predominate.
Longitudinal and multimodal studies, external validation, demographic fairness
and prospective intervention testing are needed before translating predictive
dietary signals into personalized mental-health support. Systematic review
registration https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261320333,
identifier CRD420261320333.
