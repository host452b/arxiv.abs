Title: Physical Activity and Exercise, Novel Therapies and Technologies in Adolescents with Type 1 Diabetes
Authors: Rebecca Tanja Zimmer
ID: openalex.W7236075405
URL: https://doi.org/10.15495/epub_ubt_00009721

Abstract:
Regular physical activity provides well-established benefits for individuals
with type 1 diabetes (T1D), including improved insulin sensitivity,
cardiovascular health, and psychological wellbeing. However, exercise is also
associated with rapid glycaemic fluctuations and an increased risk of
dysglycaemia. This risk is particularly pronounced in adolescents, as pubertal
physiology and irregular activity patterns complicate glycaemic management.
Accordingly, this cumulative dissertation aims to advance the understanding of
exercise in adolescents with T1D, with a focus on safety, efficacy, and the role
of diabetes technologies in supporting glycaemic control. The first study, a
randomized controlled trial (ChilDFiT1), evaluated a 4-week structured track and
field training intervention combined with intensive glycaemic management in
adolescents with T1D. Compared with intensive glycaemic management alone, the
intervention resulted in significant improvements in key glycaemic outcomes,
demonstrating the safety and feasibility of structured exercise in this
population. The second study assessed the performance of real-time continuous
glucose monitoring (rtCGM) before, during, and after exercise sessions. While
overall accuracy was acceptable, sensor reliability declined during rapid
glucose changes, particularly during hypoglycaemia, underscoring the need for
cautious, context-aware interpretation of CGM data around physical activity. The
third study synthesized current evidence on automated insulin delivery (AID)
systems in the context of exercise. While AID systems effectively improve
glycaemic outcomes when physical activity is announced, unannounced exercise
remains a significant limitation, highlighting the need for further
technological adaptation. Taken together, these studies provide an integrative
perspective on the interplay between exercise physiology, glucose monitoring,
and diabetes technology in adolescent T1D. By combining clinical evidence,
methodological evaluation, and technological analysis, this dissertation
establishes a comprehensive framework to support safe exercise participation
among this vulnerable population. The findings demonstrate that structured
exercise can safely and effectively improve glycaemic outcomes in adolescents,
with direct implications for clinical exercise counselling to support informed,
confident, and individualized care. Overall, the work provides a coherent
foundation for future research and for integrating exercise therapy and diabetes
technology into paediatric T1D management.
