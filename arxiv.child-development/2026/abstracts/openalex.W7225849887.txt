Title: Teaching quality and social-emotional competence in kindergarten STEM classrooms: differential associations across children's initial competence
Authors: Jingfang Zhang, Kamariah Abu Bakar, Nurazidawati Mohamad Arsad
ID: openalex.W7225849887
URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2026.2016018

Abstract:
Introduction Social-emotional competence (SEC) is a key part of early
development. It is shaped by classroom interactions. Kindergarten STEM
activities may provide rich environments for problem solving, collaboration,
communication and regulation. Children enter classrooms with different levels of
SEC. It is unclear whether developmental starting points are associated with
different rates of change or condition the association between teaching quality
and subsequent SEC. Studies have focused on average associations between
classroom quality and social emotional development, with less attention to
differences between instructional contexts. We examined whether initial SEC was
associated with different developmental rates and whether it conditionality of
the association between teaching quality and subsequent SEC across STEM and
regular instruction. Methods We conducted longitudinal quasi-experiment with 224
children 5–6 years in 12 classrooms in 3 public kindergartens in Hanzhong,
China. Six classrooms participated in an 8 week STEM activity consisting of 16
engineering design activities, and six classrooms continued regular instruction.
SEC was assessed at 4 time points by DESSA-mini, and teaching quality was
measured at 3 time points by cognitive activation, supportive climate, and
classroom management. Longitudinal mixed effects models examined developmental
progression and whether baseline SEC moderated the gap between teaching quality
and subsequent SEC. Results Baseline SEC predicts subsequent SEC levels
strongly, but not timewise. There is no statistical evidence that changes are
different. However, there is a significant three-way association between
instructional condition, baseline SEC and overall teaching quality indicating
that the association differs between developmental starting points and
instructional settings, but not for individual teaching quality dimensions.
Discussion Our results indicate developmental continuity of SEC levels with
broadly similar rates of change. We note that developmental starting points may
influence the relationship between overall teaching quality and subsequent SEC.
We consider this pattern as qualified evidence of developmentally differed
association rather than as a compensatory effect. These results highlight the
importance of distinguishing developmental level, developmental rate and
responsiveness to classroom processes and call for more research on teaching-
quality configurations, child engagement, and instructional adaptation.
