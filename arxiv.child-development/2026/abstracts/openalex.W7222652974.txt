Title: Digital Mental Health Supports for Young People
Authors: Carmen Kealy, Adele McLoughlin, Alba Madrid‐Cagigal, Jamie McNulty, Siobhan M. O’Neill, Gary J Donohoe, Maurice D. Mulvenna, Margaret Barry
ID: openalex.W7222652974
URL: https://doi.org/10.21251/bbeb8c2b-5902-4a01-8c46-1e0f26ecb8f6

Abstract:
Young people across the island of Ireland are navigating rising levels of
psychological distress in a context marked by long waiting lists, fragmented
services, and persistent inequities. Digital mental health supports offer a
promising way to expand access, but international and local evidence shows that
these tools are often underused when they are not co-produced with young people
or tailored to diverse cultural and social realities. As part of the larger
Atlantic Futures project, this all-island research study set out to address that
gap by engaging young people—particularly those experiencing marginalisation—and
the staff who support them to identify needs, evaluate digital tools, and
generate evidence to guide implementation. Drawing on scoping reviews, surveys,
creative workshops, a co-designed Google Site, and an acceptability and
implementation study with a combined number of approximately 150 marginalised
young people in the ROI and NI, the project found low awareness of existing
digital supports among young people but interest in accessible, relatable, and
youth-centred tools. Young people emphasised anonymity, immediacy, and autonomy
as key advantages of digital supports but stressed that credibility, cultural
relevance, and simplicity are essential for sustained engagement. Tools backed
by trusted institutions (e.g., HSE, Action Mental Health) and those reviewed by
peers were perceived as most reliable. Youth participants consistently rejected
overly clinical, complex, or “school-like” designs, favouring platforms that are
intuitive, strengths-based, and personalised. Across methods, young people
described mental health challenges rooted in structural pressures—academic
stress, financial strain, stigma, discrimination, and rural isolation.
Marginalised groups, including Traveller youth, migrants, LGBTQ+ young people,
and those with disabilities, reported additional barriers to help-seeking.
Digital tools were seen as valuable complements to traditional services,
particularly where waiting lists, geography, or stigma limit access. However,
young people were clear that digital supports cannot replace human connection;
instead, they should form part of blended, flexible pathways that integrate
online and offline care. Consultation with service providers echoed these
insights, highlighting the need for evidence-based tools, strong data
protection, simple navigation, and organisational training to support
implementation. They also identified literacy, digital skills, and privacy
concerns as barriers for young people, reinforcing the importance of low-
threshold, anonymous, and accessible design. The findings from an all-island
policy dialogue with key policymakers, service providers and young people
underscored the absence of a unified all-island governance framework for youth
digital mental health and called for coordinated, youth-led approaches to
accreditation, implementation, and participation. Overall, the project
demonstrates that co-produced, equity-orientated digital mental health supports
can meaningfully enhance access and engagement for young people across Ireland.
To maximise impact, future efforts should prioritise youth participation,
cultural competence, implementation planning, and cross-sector collaboration.
Digital supports must be embedded within broader mental health systems—not
treated as standalone solutions—to ensure that all young people can access
timely, relevant, and trustworthy care.
