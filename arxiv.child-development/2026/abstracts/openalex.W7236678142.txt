Title: The Impact of Parental Migration on Child Nutrition: A Comparative Study of Indonesia and the Philippines
Authors: Yao Fu, Yumeng Du, Xiaochen Zhou, Lucy Porter Jordan
ID: openalex.W7236678142
URL: https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-11545359/v1

Abstract:
Background Parental migration is increasingly recognized as an important social
determinant of child health in low- and middle-income countries, yet existing
evidence remains mixed and is largely derived from cross-sectional studies. This
study examined the longitudinal relationship between parental migration and
child nutritional outcomes in two major migration-sending countries in Southeast
Asia, Indonesia and the Philippines, with particular attention to the gender of
the migrant parent and the timing of children’s exposure to migration. Methods
We analyzed longitudinal data from the Child Health and Migrant Parents in
South‑East Asia (CHAMPSEA) project collected in 2008–2009 and 2016–2017. The
analytic sample comprised children aged 3–5 years at baseline and 10–14 years at
follow‑up. Anthropometric data were converted into z‑scores for height‑for‑age
(HAZ), weight‑for‑age (WAZ), and body mass index‑for‑age (BAZ). Fixed‑effects
models were used to estimate the impact of parental migration type on changes in
nutritional status, while multivariable logistic regression models assessed
whether early exposure to paternal or maternal migration was associated with
child nutrition outcomes in later life. Results In Indonesia, mother‑involved
migration was associated with higher BAZ over time but not with HAZ, whereas no
significant average effects of parental migration on either BAZ or HAZ were
observed in the Philippines. Among Indonesian children, early exposure to
paternal migration was linked to a lower risk of undernutrition but a higher
risk of overweight in early adolescence. Among Filipino children, paternal
migration before age 3 was associated with an increased risk of children’s
stunting at ages 3 to 5. Household structural characteristics, including the
number of older and younger siblings and the presence of co‑resident
grandparents, were also significantly associated with child nutritional
outcomes. Conclusions Exposure to parental migration during early life
influences children’s long‑term nutritional trajectories, with effects varying
by country context, parent gender, and developmental stage. The potential
nutritional benefits of parental migration can be leveraged through
interventions implemented at critical developmental periods and by incorporating
nutrition‑literacy support for stay‑behind caregivers.
