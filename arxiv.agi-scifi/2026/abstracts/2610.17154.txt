Title: Neuromorphic Decoding and Dynamical Image Reconstruction from Populations of Spiking Retinal Ganglion Cells
Authors: Ning Zhang, Arto Nurmikko
ID: 2610.17154
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17154

Abstract:
Decoding visual stimuli from the spiking activity of retinal ganglion cells
(RGCs) informs both our understanding of the retinal code and the design of
visual prostheses. Natural vision is dynamic, yet state-of-the-art decoders
mostly reconstruct single frames with artificial neural networks that process
dense, continuous-valued signals rather than the retina's sparse, asynchronous
spikes. We asked whether dynamic scenes can be reconstructed and tracked
entirely within the spike domain, and at what computational cost. We built an
end-to-end spiking neural network (SNN) whose neurons integrate the recorded RGC
spikes over time. Populations of up to 1836 RGCs were recorded ex vivo from
mouse retina on high-density microelectrode arrays while flashed images, moving
bars, drifting gratings and randomly moving handwritten digits were presented.
For moving digits, one shared spiking encoder fed two readouts, one
reconstructing the image and one decoding the digit's position. Compute energy
was estimated from counted synaptic operations. The SNN decoded what and where
at once. It reconstructed the stimuli with a structural similarity (SSIM) of up
to 0.93, refining the reconstruction as spikes accumulated, and tracked a
randomly moving target with a mean error of 1.33 pixels on a $32\times32$ grid.
Its fidelity nearly matched that of the best of 17 larger spiking and artificial
decoders (5.9 million to 1.7 billion parameters, versus 5.4 million), and at a
mean firing rate of 14.2% it needed an estimated $\sim$70-fold less compute
energy than a dense artificial network of the same architecture. Dynamic visual
scenes can thus be decoded from retinal population activity in the spike domain,
with a compact network and at a compute cost compatible with implantable and
wearable neural interfaces, supporting spike-native decoders for visual
prostheses.
