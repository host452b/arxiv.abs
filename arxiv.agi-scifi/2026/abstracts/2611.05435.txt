Title: From Earth's Harshest Landscapes to Lunar Horizons: Engineering NEA Telescopes for Extreme Environments
Authors: M. Hadjara, A. Pomazan, H. Ji, Q. Dong, J. Zheng, M. Yindun, L. Cament, M. Adams, M. Diaz, R. Mendez et al.
ID: 2611.05435
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05435

Abstract:
Near-Earth Asteroids (NEAs) represent both a scientific opportunity and a
potential threat to human civilization. While current survey programs have
successfully detected most large NEAs, a significant observational gap remains
for objects smaller than approximately 140 m, which can still produce regional-
scale devastation. Ground-based surveys are intrinsically limited by atmospheric
conditions, weather, and solar avoidance constraints, which leave a significant
fraction of near-Sun objects difficult to detect. In particular, Earth-based
observatories remain largely blind to objects approaching from the Sun-ward
direction, reducing available warning times. Future lunar observatories offer a
promising solution to this limitation. A telescope operating on the Moon could
enable observations of regions inaccessible from Earth, provide enhanced
sensitivity, and significantly improve early detection capabilities. However,
the lunar environment imposes severe engineering constraints, including thermal
cycling, dust contamination, radiation exposure, and autonomous operation
requirements. We propose the AMAS(Atacama-Mongolia-Antarctica-Sahara) project,
an international initiative using Earth's most extreme environments as analogue
sites for future lunar astronomical facilities. By deploying and testing
specialized NEA survey telescopes in these environments, key technologies for
future lunar observatories can be validated under realistic operational
conditions. This paper presents the scientific rationale, engineering
challenges, site selection strategy, and long-term roadmap toward a global
Earth-Moon network for planetary defense.
