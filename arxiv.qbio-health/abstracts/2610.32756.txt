Title: Video-rate quantitative phase and polarimetric imaging (QP2I) for label-free imaging of contracting muscle cells
Authors: Lucas Kreiss Roohian, Nachiket Pathak, David Ripp, Mitchell Harling, Nils M. Kronenberg, Marcel Schubert, Malte C. Gather
ID: 2610.32756
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32756

Abstract:
Quantitative phase imaging (QPI) has become a standard approach for label-free
microscopy of transparent specimens, mapping the optical path length. However,
the isotropic phase is blind to the molecular order underlying optical
anisotropy. Implementations that also add quantitative contrast to birefringence
rely on multi-frame illumination or polarization-state scanning, or on dedicated
interferometric hardware and thus trade away acquisition speed or the simplicity
of a standard microscope. We present Quantitative Phase and Polarimetric Imaging
(QPPI), a computational imaging framework that converts a standard differential
interference contrast (DIC) microscope with a polarization camera into a single-
shot, video-rate, dual-contrast imaging system. We developed a new globally
regularized phase reconstruction that eliminates artifacts of prior DIC-to-QPI
approaches, providing quantitative phase information, and used per-pixel Stokes-
based reconstruction to obtain quantitative anisotropic retardance. We validate
the approach across simulations, static reference targets, fixed muscle tissue
and living dynamically moving cells. QPPI enables all-optical and label-free
studies of contractile dynamics in live cardiomyocytes. It provides both
structural and functional readouts at sufficient frame rates to resolve fast
cardiomyocyte contractions. Conventional fluorescent labels bleach over time,
perturb physiology, and preclude long-term monitoring. Instead, QPPI enables
long-term, label-free interrogation of dynamic cellular processes at
quantitative contrast and provides structural readouts similar to those provided
by established fluorescent labels, such as frame-by-frame sarcomere length. It
also offers previously inaccessible measurements, like quantitative analysis of
retardance changes during muscle contraction, which provides a direct optical
signature of muscle photoelasticity.
